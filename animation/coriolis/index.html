The Physics of Free Fall in Artificial Gravity

1. The External Perspective (Inertial Frame)

To an observer floating outside the space station in microgravity, when you jump or throw an object from the floor, you are already moving sideways at the rim's tangential speed ($v_{\text{rim}} = \omega R$).

Once your feet leave the floor, no gravity or external forces act on you (assuming negligible air resistance). You travel in a perfectly straight line at constant velocity across the chord of the circle until the curved floor rotates back up to meet you.

\vec{r}(t) = \vec{r}_0 + (\vec{v}_{\text{rim}} + \vec{v}_{\text{rel}})\,t

2. The Internal Perspective (Rotating Frame & Coriolis Effect)

To an inhabitant standing inside the rotating cylinder, "down" is radially outward (centrifugal force). When you jump straight up toward the central hub:

  • You retain your initial linear speed ($v_{\text{rim}}$).
  • As your distance from the hub ($r$) decreases during your jump, the local floor beneath you requires less tangential speed ($\omega r$) to keep up.
  • Because your tangential velocity exceeds the rotational speed at smaller radii, you appear to accelerate spinward (forward).
  • This apparent sideways deflection is the Coriolis acceleration: $\vec{a}_{\text{coriolis}} = -2(\vec{\omega} \times \vec{v}_{\text{rel}})$.

3. Answers to the 1 km Ring Question

In a $1\text{ km}$ diameter habitat ($R = 500\text{ m}$) spinning at $1.34\text{ RPM}$ to produce $1.0\text{g}$ of artificial gravity, a human jumping straight up at $3.0\text{ m/s}$ (approx. $0.46\text{ m}$ / $1.5\text{ ft}$ vertical leap) will be airborne for ~0.61 seconds and will land approximately 0.14 meters (5.5 inches) spinward (forward) of the spot they jumped from.

In smaller centrifuges (such as $R = 10\text{ m}$), the effect is vastly amplified—the jumper will land several meters ahead of their launch spot or may even trip if not prepared!
